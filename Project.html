More Inputs, Less Profit

A cost–benefit analysis of organic and integrated farming for smallholder leafy vegetablesA single comparison of two plots over one season, using expected yields

A learning project. It compares costs and expected yields for two farming systems over one season, so I treat the results as practice and a starting point, not as proof.

The section What I learned sums up what I took from working through it.

The question

Does adding fertiliser and pesticide to a manure-based system pay for itself?

The organic system uses land, manure, labour and seed. The integrated system uses all of that plus fertiliser and pesticide. Both grow amaranth and corchorus, two common African leafy vegetables.

Organic here means manure only, with no synthetic fertiliser or pesticide. It is not certified organic.

Method

I used three standard cost–benefit measures. I did not use NPV or IRR because the data covers a single season, so there are no future cash flows to discount.

MeasureFormula
Net benefitTotal revenue − total cost
Return on investment (ROI)Net benefit ÷ total cost × 100
Benefit–cost ratio (BCR)Total revenue ÷ total cost

Assumptions held constant: a fixed price of ₦200/kg for both systems (based on market findings, in 2025 prices), one production cycle, the same labour cost, average weather, no post-harvest losses, and no inflation or price changes.

Working through the numbers

Organic

Net benefit = 50,000 − 41,700 = 8,300

ROI = 8,300 ÷ 41,700 × 100 = 19.9%

BCR = 50,000 ÷ 41,700 = 1.20

Integrated

Net benefit = 42,000 − 51,450 = −9,450

ROI = −9,450 ÷ 51,450 × 100 = −18.4%

BCR = 42,000 ÷ 51,450 = 0.82

What the baseline shows

  • Integrated cost ₦9,750 more to run (23% more), all of it from fertiliser (₦6,000) and pesticide (₦3,750).
  • It is expected to produce 40kg less: 210kg against 250kg, or 16% lower. Expected revenue is ₦8,000 lower as a result.
  • Cost per kg was about ₦167 for organic and ₦245 for integrated, against a selling price of ₦200. Integrated needed roughly 257kg to break even and is expected to produce 210kg. That is 47kg, or 22.5%, above its expected yield, just to cover its costs.
  • Covering costs is not the same as competing with organic. To match organic’s ₦8,300 net benefit, integrated would need about 299kg, which is 89kg or 42% above its expected yield. This is the figure the comparison with organic actually requires.

What I can’t conclude

The figures show that the integrated system is expected to lose money. They do not show why its expected yield is lower. Fertiliser and pesticide may have little effect, or the plots may differ in soil, pests, planting date or management. Because the yields are expected rather than measured, I can’t separate the effect of the system from the effect of the plot.

Stress-testing the result

I recalculated the result under two shocks and under both together. A 20% price drop means ₦160/kg. A 30% manure increase takes manure from ₦25,000 to ₦32,500.

What I learned

  • Work out the numbers for every scenario before saying what happens. Organic only makes a profit if the price stays above about ₦167/kg. A 20% drop takes the price to ₦160/kg, so organic loses money. I only saw this after I did the sums.
  • Expected figures show what to expect, not why. The integrated system is expected to lose money, but the data can’t tell me why its expected yield is lower.
  • If a cost is the same in both systems, like labour and manure, check it. It may be hiding a real difference.

Limits

  • One season of expected yields, not measured harvests, with no repeats, so the result is indicative only.
  • Plot size is not known, so yields can’t be compared per unit of land.
  • Labour is ₦10,000 in both systems, but applying fertiliser and pesticide probably takes extra time. Integrated cost may be understated.
  • Manure is ₦25,000 in both systems and is about 60% of organic’s cost. I have not separated bought manure from manure a farm makes itself, and unpaid family labour is not costed.
  • Both systems use the same ₦200/kg price, based on market findings. Organic produce may sell at a premium, and real prices change.
  • Post-harvest losses are excluded. If they differ between systems, the comparison changes.
  • Livelihoods and environmental effects were not measured. The question relates to SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 12 (Responsible Consumption and Production), but this study does not measure either.

What I would do next

  • Measure actual harvests and compare them with the expected yields.
  • Repeat the comparison across several plots and seasons, so differences can be tested rather than just observed.
  • Record plot size and report cost and yield per unit area.
  • Check prices across seasons and markets, including any organic premium.
  • Cost family labour and farm-made manure.
  • Collect household income data before saying anything about livelihoods.
